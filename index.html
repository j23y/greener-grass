Notes and Sources

These results are for fun and largely based on estimates and decisions by the creator and Claude. Sorry for any mistakes or oversights!

NOTES

  • City limits. Each city is its official limits, so nearby towns like Santa Monica, Miami Beach or Bethesda are left out, and suburbs and rural areas aren't offered. Cities that are a small core of their metro can look worse on crime than they feel; a small adjustment softens this.
  • Not in the game: childcare, family and friends, air quality, and local versus chain businesses. Household income counts toward what is left over.
  • Estimates. Some numbers are my own estimates, including miles driven, the emergency savings amount, moving travel costs and Jinxy's hunch.

METHODOLOGY

  • The cities. The 48 largest US cities, St. Louis and Tallahassee, plus 16 well-known smaller cities: Tampa, Orlando, New Orleans, Pittsburgh, Cincinnati, Cleveland, Salt Lake City, Honolulu, Richmond, Buffalo, Providence, Birmingham, Madison, Boise, Charleston and Durham. Honolulu is the Census Bureau's urban Honolulu area, not all of Oahu.
  • Your neighborhood. The census tracts where households your age and income cluster most. Rent, density, commuting, Walk Score and businesses are measured there; everything else is city-wide.
  • Your pay. By default every city uses your current salary. Estimated salary instead scales it by what full-time workers in your industry earn (the median, from the Census Bureau's American Community Survey) in each metro compared with your current metro, or the nation if your city isn't on the list. The rest of your household's income moves with each metro's overall pay level.
  • Rent. Cheap, average and comfortable are the low, middle and high end of rents in your neighborhood, raised to reflect what newer renters pay. Today's asking rents can still be higher.
  • Taxes. 2026 federal, state and city income taxes for a single filer, plus each state's average sales tax.
  • Retirement. Your answer sets how much of what is left after expenses you save, up to the 2026 limits. It goes to a 401(k) first, then a traditional IRA, lowering your taxes. It counts as an expense in what you see, but cities are still ranked on retirement savings plus what is left over.
  • Transportation. A car's cost is insurance, gas (a five-year average), upkeep, parking and, if needed, buying one. Going car-free costs part of that, less where many people live without a car, and nearly the full amount plus a penalty where almost no one does.
  • Essentials. What a typical person living alone spends, not the average, adjusted for local prices, plus a small emergency savings cushion. For bigger households, rent and spending (but not car costs) grow with the square root of household size, a standard OECD scale. The thriftier your spending habits, the closer this moves to what thrifty people spend. Health costs follow each metro's HCCI spending index; Sacramento, Honolulu, Birmingham and Madison have none, so they use the national level.
  • Fun stuff. Restaurants and other outings, based on how often you go out and local prices. Each time out assumes about $20 of food (my own estimate) plus one drink at the national median price of $6.52, adjusted for local prices.
  • Moving. A one-time cost in the first year that grows with distance.
  • Your city never goes below $0. If your current city would come out negative, spending is trimmed until it doesn't, starting with fun stuff. Other cities get the same trims.
  • Your kind of neighbors. Rates how mixed each neighborhood and city is by race and ethnicity, income and age. All 66 cities are fairly mixed, so high, average and lower diversity are relative to each other.
  • Your field. How strong, big and fast-growing your industry is in each metro. It counts for more with estimated salary.
  • Safety. City-wide crime rates. Atlanta, Jacksonville, Tallahassee, New Orleans and Orlando use the closest available figures because the main FBI table skips them. Honolulu's police figures cover all of Oahu.
  • Restaurants and culture. Mostly how many are nearby per square mile, mixed with acclaim: 2026 James Beard Award semifinalists in the city and the metro's rank in the 2025 Arts Vibrancy Index. Acclaim counts more if you go out often or like big cities.
  • Parks and nature. A mix of city parks and nature within 50 miles of the city center. City parks are the share of residents within a 10-minute walk of a park and the share of land that is parkland, from ParkScore, or from TPL's ParkServe (the same measures) for cities ParkScore leaves out. Nature mixes how much land is public and open to visitors (national and state forests, parks, refuges, BLM land and more, from USGS PAD-US 4.1) with how much of the area is ocean or large lakes (Natural Earth). Small lakes and rivers aren't counted.
  • Weather and walkability. Weather uses average temperatures and sunshine. Walkability blends car-free households, walking and transit commuting, grocery stores and Walk Score. In a few added-city neighborhoods Walk Score only had a single-point score that was implausibly low for how dense they are (downtown Boise scored 0), so Walk Score is left out there.
  • Schools. Only counts for households of three or more, and counts more the bigger the household. Average 3rd to 8th grade test scores in the main public school district named for the city, 2009–2019, from the Stanford Education Data Archive. Some cities have several districts, and scores also reflect students' family backgrounds, not just the schools. Honolulu uses Hawaii's single statewide district.
  • City size. Compares the city's population and its metro's, from the Census Bureau (ACS 2020–2024). Your size answer also nudges how much restaurants, culture and things to do count, a little less for small, a little more for big. Neighborhood energy is scored separately, so a small city with a lively neighborhood can still rank well.
  • Politics. Based on who won the city's county (all five boroughs for New York) in the 2024 presidential election, not the city itself. Cities that voted the other way lose a little if you lean and a lot if you are solid. No preference changes nothing.
  • Pro sports. Teams playing within 30 miles of the city, with the major leagues (NFL, NBA, MLB, NHL, MLS) counting most, then the WNBA, NWSL and PWHL, then top minor leagues (Wikipedia's list of US pro teams, Nov 2026). It only matters if you want a lively neighborhood or a big city, and even then only a little.
  • Jinxy's hunch. My own read of each city's feel. It is a small part of the score.
  • The score. Each measure ranks the city among the 66, and your answers set how much each counts. Cities you can't afford lose points. What people search about a city nudges the score slightly.
  • Counting. The site counts visits with GoatCounter, which keeps no personal information. When you finish, it also counts your current city and your top 25 matches, and nothing else about your answers.
  • Rounding and edits. Dollar amounts round to the nearest $100. Editing your current city's receipt changes that line in every city by the same proportion.
